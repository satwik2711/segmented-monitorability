Segmenting Monitorability

Chain-of-thought monitors can read the reasoning trace. But the same trace can be represented in very different ways. This post asks whether exposing coherent semantic turns can help a monitor localize evidence, track how it evolves, and catch it earlier—without changing a single word of the trace.

Satwik Pandey, September 2026. About 20 minutes.

TL;DR

  • CoT monitoring has established that reasoning traces can contain useful supervisory signal. What is much less settled is how the same trace should be represented to the monitor.
  • Segmentation does not create information. Its possible value is as an inductive bias: expose where evidence appears, whether it persists, and how the reasoning changes over time.
  • The obvious finer units solve different problems. Splitting on epistemic cue words produced 81.7 chunks per trace and severe fragmentation in a 100-trace audit; fixed four-sentence windows were 93.5% well-formed. The weakness of fixed windows is not readability—their cuts simply ignore what the reasoning is doing.
  • Adaptive semantic turns are one candidate representation. In a separate paired audit over 2,082 traces, they produced fewer, larger units and almost eliminated tiny chunks, while also exposing a real over-merging tail.
  • The actual hypothesis is downstream. Holding the trace, monitor, prompt, and token budget fixed, semantically aligned boundaries should improve localization, lead time, or robustness relative to fixed and randomized boundaries. They do not need to improve final AUROC to be useful.
  • The mechanism here is deliberately cheap. Score sentence boundaries by next-token predictive entropy, convert it to an effective branching factor, smooth the sequence, and cut at unusually high local peaks subject to a minimum-length constraint.

Epistemic status: a position plus a working prototype. The evidence is exploratory: a rubric-based chunk-quality audit on 100 traces, a paired segmentation audit over 2,082 exact-coverage traces from nine stored runs, and a hand analysis of 20 MMLU traces from Qwen3-4B-Thinking-2507. None of this shows that semantic chunking improves a monitor. Everything here is black-box analysis of text and log-probabilities, not mechanistic interpretability.

Where CoT monitoring stands

Reasoning models externalize part of their computation as text. The old scratchpad framing is useful here: chain of thought is not a transcript of hidden cognition, but a working surface on which the model can write intermediate state, try a route, revise it, and carry information forward. Nye et al. used exactly this idea to make multi-step computation easier for language models.1

For monitoring, the important fact is that this working surface can expose behavior that is hard to infer from the final action alone. Baker et al. showed this concretely in reward-hacking environments: giving a monitor access to the agent's chain of thought substantially improved detection over watching actions and outputs alone.2 That result gives us the first premise of this post: there can be safety-relevant signal in the trace.

Korbak et al. turn that observation into a broader safety argument. They describe CoT monitorability as a new and fragile opportunity: natural-language reasoning gives us an unusually direct oversight surface, but future training choices, data, or scaling could make that surface less usable.12 The point I take from this is not simply that “monitorability matters.” It is that the usefulness of the CoT depends on the interface through which we read it.

Guan et al. make monitorability operational. They define it as a monitor's ability to predict properties of an agent's behavior and organize evaluations into intervention, process, and outcome-property archetypes.13 The process evaluations are especially relevant here: experts enumerate valid solution paths and the monitor is asked which steps the model actually took. In other words, local reasoning structure already matters to the evaluation target—even though the representation of the trace itself is usually treated as plumbing rather than as an experimental variable.

Dilgren and Wiegreffe's critique of the intervention evaluations exposes a related granularity problem at the level of labels. When an intervention's causal effect is only identified across a set of sampled rollouts, we do not know which particular rollouts were actually changed by the intervention; a monitor can therefore receive credit without necessarily firing on the same rollouts that carried the causal effect.14 That is not an argument for semantic chunking by itself. But it is a strong argument for localization: a monitor verdict is more useful when we can tie it to the particular rollout—and ideally the particular span—that supports it.

Emmons et al.'s distinction between legibility and coverage helps draw the boundary of what segmentation could plausibly accomplish.3 A better representation might improve legibility by organizing evidence already present in the trace. It cannot manufacture coverage: if the relevant reasoning was never verbalized, no boundary rule will recover it.

The second premise comes from work on the internal structure of the trace itself. Thought Anchors is especially relevant because it refuses to treat every sentence as equally important. Bogdan et al. resample around individual sentences and measure their counterfactual effect on the final answer; a small set of planning and backtracking sentences have outsized downstream influence.4 This is a concrete reason to care about locality: useful information is not uniformly distributed along a reasoning trace.

ReasoningFlow pushes the same idea toward representation. It builds fine-grained discourse structures over long reasoning traces, exposing local operations such as verification, self-reflection, assumptions, and backtracking, and studies how these structures relate to reasoning-trace monitorability.5 Just as importantly, its discourse graph should not be mistaken for the model's hidden causal graph. That is the right warning for this post too: a semantic turn is a textual unit that may help monitoring, not a claim that we have recovered the underlying computation.

Put together, these results leave a fairly natural gap. CoT monitoring asks whether useful evidence can be read from a trace; reasoning-structure work shows that the trace has non-uniform local organization. The representation layer between those two questions is comparatively underexplored.

The granularity problem

Suppose a long trace frames the task, tries one route, hits a contradiction, repairs it, verifies the new route, and commits. A whole-rollout monitor receives every one of those words. In an information-theoretic sense, adding boundaries does not give it new evidence.

So why segment at all? Because real monitors are finite models with finite context, attention, and prompting budgets. A representation can change the inductive bias of the monitoring problem: which spans are presented together, which transitions become explicit, and whether the system has a natural coordinate for saying “the first warning appeared here.”

This matters most when the monitoring objective has a temporal component. An early warning and a late warning can have identical episode-level AUROC and very different operational value. A suspicious idea that appears once and is then rejected is different from one that survives every later revision. A single rollout score can represent both cases, but it does not naturally expose the difference.

The question is therefore not “does segmentation reveal secret information?” It is narrower: what is the cheapest representation that preserves the local structure a monitor may want to use? I started with two obvious answers—epistemic cue words and fixed sentence windows—because both are simple enough to falsify quickly.

Epistemic verbalization is a signal, not a boundary

Reasoning models narrate changes in their own stance constantly: wait, but, actually, let me check, therefore. I will call this epistemic verbalization: language about the status of the current belief or plan rather than the task content itself. These phrases can be useful monitor features. In SELFDOUBT, for example, hedging and verification language carries uncertainty signal in a single trace.6 My first mistake was to assume that a useful feature should also make a useful delimiter.

They don't. In a 100-trace audit (88 with non-empty reasoning), Wait appeared 1,672 times and But 1,600 times. Splitting at markers like these produced 81.7 chunks per trace. Under the audit rubric, 54.3% of those chunks were one-word or near-empty fragments, 62.9% had little or no value as embedding units, and only 19.6% were well-formed. The chunks looked like this:

Wait, maybe it's a different number
But the problem says characters are…
Actually, I should check one more case

The trouble is that these words are context-dependent. Wait can open a genuine change of hypothesis, or precede a one-token arithmetic fix. But can reverse an argument, or just join two clauses. The word is a feature of the reasoning; the span around it tells you what happened.

Structural markers are much better. Explicit cues such as Step 2, Therefore, or In summary produced only 7.4 chunks per trace and 92.8% well-formed chunks in the same audit. I would happily use them when the model writes in a visibly structured style. The limitation is different: they detect declared structure. Free-form reasoning can pivot without announcing the pivot, and harmless style changes can remove the cue while leaving the underlying move intact.

The lesson I took is a small reframe. Epistemic verbalizations are features, not delimiters. They are exactly the kind of thing a monitor may want to score inside a chunk—hedging, revision, verification, commitment—but the span around the cue tells us whether anything important actually changed.

Sentence windows are a strong baseline—and semantically agnostic

The obvious fix is to split on sentences and group them into fixed windows. This works well, and it's important to say so plainly. Four-sentence windows gave about 20 chunks per trace, 63 words per chunk, and 93.5% well-formed, with no fragments at all. Any adaptive method has to beat this, not the marker strawman.

It helps to be precise about what that 93.5% measures. The audit rubric asks whether each chunk is well-formed: a complete thought, with enough content, at a sensible length. It never asks whether a chunk's edges sit where the reasoning changes direction. Sentence windows score well on the first question almost by construction, and the rubric is silent on the second. That second question, does the cut land on a turn?, is the one this post is about, and it is the empty column in Figure 2.

If sentence windows have a weakness, it lies on that second axis. Their boundaries are syntactic rather than epistemic. A four-sentence window cuts because four sentences have gone by. It does not care whether sentence four raised the contradiction and sentence five resolved it. In other words, fixed windows regularize length, not meaning. They can fuse two reasoning moves that happen to fit in one window, or split a single argument just as it becomes interpretable. The chunks are grammatical; their timing is indifferent to the reasoning.

The larger paired audit sharpens the point. The adaptive rule is not simply making chunks “better”; it is moving the operating point. It compresses the trace into fewer units and almost eliminates tiny spans, but sometimes merges too aggressively. That is exactly why chunk quality and boundary quality should be evaluated separately. A chunk can be perfectly readable and still contain two different reasoning moves.

The position

Semantic segmentation is not itself “monitorability.” It is a candidate representation layer for CoT monitoring: a way to expose the local structure of evidence that is already present in the trace.

A useful intermediate representation

This distinction matters. CoT monitorability is a property of how much useful information a monitor can recover from a reasoning trace. CoT monitoring is the actual system that reads the trace and makes a judgment. Semantic segmentation sits between them: it changes how the same trace is presented to that system.

For an ideal monitor with unlimited context, adding boundaries should not create information. The interesting hypothesis is more practical: well-placed boundaries may be a useful inductive bias for a finite monitor. They provide coordinates for local evidence, let the monitor revisit coherent spans rather than arbitrary token ranges, and make temporal questions—first appearance, persistence, revision—natural outputs instead of post-hoc analyses.

The hypothesis to test

Hold the trace, monitor, prompt, and token budget fixed. If semantic boundaries are useful, they should improve localization, lead time, or robustness relative to fixed and randomized boundaries—even if the final rollout-level classification score barely changes.

Sentence windows are therefore not a strawman; they are the baseline to beat. If randomizing the semantic boundaries leaves results unchanged, or if four-sentence windows perform just as well, the extra machinery has not earned its complexity.

A mechanism: cut where the next step branches

The prototype takes a deliberately conservative route. Sentence boundaries remain the only legal places to cut, because punctuation is useful scaffolding. The chunker's job is to decide which sentence boundaries coincide with a meaningful change in the model's local predictive state. Statistically, this is a lightweight change-point detection problem over a one-dimensional uncertainty signal.

An earlier version averaged the observed-token surprisal within each sentence. The current version asks a different question at the boundary itself: right as the next sentence begins, how many continuations does the model consider plausible?

At each candidate boundary, read the model's top-20 next-token log-probabilities given everything before it. Renormalize them into probabilities pi and compute

H = − Σi pi log2 pi,     B = 2H

H is predictive entropy: uncertainty over what the model could write next. B turns that entropy into an effective branching factor—the number of equally likely choices that would have the same entropy. A sharp distribution gives a low score; a flat distribution gives a high score. Because only the top 20 continuations are available, this is a truncated proxy rather than full-vocabulary entropy.

Entropy, surprisal, perplexity. Surprisal asks how unexpected the token that actually occurred was: −log p(x). Entropy asks how uncertain the model was before seeing which token occurred: an expectation over possible next tokens. Perplexity is an exponentiated entropy or average surprisal, depending on context. The boundary signal here is closest to predictive entropy, expressed as an effective branching factor—not the perplexity of the observed sentence.

The working hypothesis is simple. While the model follows one locally stable route, the distribution over how the next sentence begins should often stay relatively concentrated. At a hard step, contradiction, or change of direction, that distribution may broaden because several continuations become locally plausible. A relative peak in branching factor is therefore a candidate change point—not proof of a reasoning turn, but a cheap place to look.

The most relevant prior result I know is the high-entropy-token work of Wang et al. They find that entropy is highly non-uniform along reasoning traces: a small minority of high-entropy tokens behave like forks that steer generation toward different reasoning paths, and RL updates concentrated on those tokens preserve much of the training benefit.7 That does not imply that a high-entropy sentence boundary is a semantic turn. It gives a narrower motivation: predictive uncertainty is a plausible place to look for branch points, and therefore a reasonable one-dimensional signal for change-point detection.

Thought Anchors provides a complementary test target rather than another justification. If branching peaks really identify consequential turns, they should correlate with sentences whose resampling has large downstream effects—especially the planning and backtracking anchors that paper identifies.4 That correlation is an experiment, not an assumption.

The rest of the mechanism is mostly about not overreacting to noisy peaks. The current implementation does the following.

  1. Split into sentences. Split on sentence-final punctuation followed by a capital letter, and on newlines, while masking about eighty common abbreviations so that "e.g." or "Dr." don't end a sentence.
  2. Window long traces. If the trace exceeds the scoring model's context, build overlapping sentence windows with about 12% token overlap. When a boundary is scored in more than one window, keep the score computed with the most left context.
  3. Score every boundary in one pass. Send each window to an OpenAI-compatible completions endpoint with echo=true, max_tokens=0, and logprobs=20. The server returns prompt log-probabilities, so no text is generated. Map each sentence boundary to its token position, falling back to the tokenizer if the server's offsets don't line up.
  4. Smooth. Take a three-boundary moving average, so a single noisy position doesn't become a new "thought".
  5. Threshold adaptively. Mark local maxima of the smoothed curve above τ = max(median + 0.5·IQR, median + 0.1), computed per trace.
  6. Glue. Cut at a marked peak only if the current chunk already holds at least 15 words, so tiny fragments never get stranded.
def branching_factor(top_logprobs):
    x = np.asarray(top_logprobs, dtype=float)
    p = np.exp(x - x.max())
    p /= p.sum()
    H = -(p * np.log2(p)).sum()
    return 2 ** H

smooth = np.convolve(boundary_scores, np.ones(3) / 3, mode="same")
median = np.median(smooth)
iqr = np.percentile(smooth, 75) - np.percentile(smooth, 25)
tau = max(median + 0.5 * iqr, median + 0.1)
peaks, _ = find_peaks(smooth, height=tau)

Two properties of Figure 5 are worth flagging because they are easy to miss in the code. First, a three-point average turns a single sharp spike into a plateau, so the cut can land a sentence away from the spike itself. Second, the threshold is set per trace, so the chunker always finds the relatively surprising boundaries. A trace with one dramatic turn will have its milder turns suppressed. Both are fixable; both are the kind of thing a boundary-quality evaluation should surface.

One design choice matters more than any constant: the chunker never rewrites the trace. It only places boundaries. A monitor should still be able to inspect the exact words the policy model produced; paraphrasing or summarizing first risks deleting the odd phrasing, contradiction, or policy-relevant detail we wanted to monitor in the first place.

The extra computation is one prompt-only forward pass per context window rather than another autoregressive generation. In practice that should usually be cheaper than producing the original trace, although the exact cost depends on model size and context length. The scoring model also does not have to be the model that wrote the trace; whether it should be is one of the open questions below.

What turns make possible

A monitor that reports when, not just whether

With turns underneath the rollout, a monitor can still emit one score, but it can also emit a short evidence path. Four quantities fall out naturally.

QuantityThe question it answers
LocalizationWhich turn first contains evidence that justifies the alarm?
Lead timeHow many turns before the relevant action does the monitor become confident?
PersistenceDoes the signal survive later revision, or was it a transient false alarm?
TransferDoes the same local pattern generalize across models, prompts, and paraphrases?

Lead time is not just a cosmetic metric. Chan et al. explicitly ask whether alignment can be predicted before a reasoning model finishes thinking, and find that useful signals can appear in early CoT segments, although activation probes outperform text-based monitors in their setting.8 Semantic turns would give text-based monitors adaptive checkpoints for asking the same temporal question without committing to a fixed token count.

A geometry for a single trace

Once a trace is split into coherent turns, there is a second possibility: embed each turn and treat the reasoning as a path e1 → e2 → … → eT through semantic space. The interesting object is not a global cluster of “good thoughts” and “bad thoughts.” It is the within-trace geometry: how far each turn moves from the last, whether the path revisits an earlier region, and whether late turns settle into a stable neighborhood. Even one rollout has shape:

MeasureDefinitionWhat it might capture
Displacement1 − cos(et, et−1)Pivots and new subproblems
RecurrenceSimilarity to a non-adjacent earlier turnCircular reconsideration
Turning angleAngle between successive displacement vectorsSharp revisions of direction
ConvergenceWhether step size shrinks and late turns clusterSettling on one route

To be clear about what this is: geometry over the externalized semantics of the scratchpad, not geometry of hidden model state. Cosine distance between two text embeddings is not a causal statement about computation. The useful first question is much weaker: do simple within-trace quantities such as recurrence or convergence predict recognizable reasoning regimes after controlling for obvious baselines like trace length?

There is a technical trap here: embedding each chunk in isolation can remove context that determines what the chunk means. Late Chunking was introduced for exactly this general failure mode in retrieval—encode the long context first, then pool the local chunk representation afterward.11 For reasoning traces, any geometric result should survive at least an isolated-embedding baseline and a context-aware embedding scheme. If the trajectory changes completely with that choice, the “geometry” was mostly an artifact of the encoder.

A first look: where hedging happens matters

To see whether temporal structure is worth keeping at all, I read 20 labelled MMLU traces from Qwen3-4B-Thinking-2507 closely: 12 correct, 6 wrong, and 2 that hadn't finished after three minutes. This is qualitative and tiny, so treat what follows as hypothesis generation.

The main takeaway is that totals don't separate good reasoning from bad, but trajectories might. Hedging and backtracking appear in correct and incorrect traces alike. Deliberate convergers hedge heavily early and then settle. Confused traces keep hedging into their final turns. Spiral divergers revisit the same options until they run out of time; one repeats "But wait" eight times and "Let me think again" five times. What distinguishes these is where the hedging sits and whether it decays, which is precisely the information a rollout-level count throws away.

The dashed box in Figure 7 is the important constraint. Two wrong answers were confident factual misrecalls: short, linear traces with no hedging and no revision, indistinguishable in shape from quick correct answers. No segmentation scheme can surface evidence the scratchpad never contained. The goal is narrower: when a trace does contain useful local dynamics, keep them in a form a monitor can use.

One more lesson from the sample was mundane. One "wrong" trace concluded the right answer several times, and the answer extractor pulled the wrong option. Label noise shows up even at n = 20, and turn-level reading is one of the easier ways to catch it.

Open problems

Everything above is a position plus a prototype, not a monitorability result. The next step is to make the representation earn its complexity. The useful experiments are the ones that can kill the idea quickly.

1. Do branching peaks track consequential steps?

This is the cheapest and strongest first test. Thought Anchors already provides sentence-level counterfactual importance from resampling: change one sentence and measure how much the downstream answer distribution moves.4 Thought Branches extends that logic by showing why a single edited continuation can be misleading and introduces repeated resampling to measure whether a reasoning step is resilient to removal.9 Score the same traces with the branching signal. If entropy peaks do not preferentially fall near high-impact or resilient planning/backtracking steps, then the boundary score is probably measuring surface uncertainty rather than reasoning structure.

2. Are these good boundaries?

Have annotators mark major reasoning transitions in a few hundred free-form traces, scoring with a tolerance window rather than exact positions. Compare fixed-token windows, sentence windows, structural markers, marker splitting, and the branching chunker on boundary precision and recall, chunk completeness, how much context each chunk needs to be interpretable, and stability under harmless paraphrase. This is the metric that would fill the empty row in Figure 2 with a real number. Design it before running the comparison, and penalize coarseness explicitly; otherwise the method that produces the fewest, longest chunks wins by default. Compare the boundary score itself against observed-token surprisal, full-vocabulary entropy, Bayesian surprise between adjacent predictive distributions, embedding distance, and a small learned classifier.

3. Do better boundaries make a better monitor?

Hold the policy trace, monitor model, prompt, total token budget, and number of monitor calls fixed; change only the boundaries. Compare whole-rollout, fixed-window, semantic, and randomized segmentation. Measure the ordinary episode-level metric, but also localization and lead time. The key kill test is simple: shift, merge, or randomize the boundaries while keeping every word unchanged. If the gains survive arbitrary boundaries, the monitor benefited from formatting or repeated prompting, not from semantics.

4. Is trace geometry real?

Compare real turn order against shuffled order and perturbed boundaries, across at least two embedding models and the three context strategies above. A geometric score is interesting only if it is stable across reasonable choices and predicts something that trace length and lexical counts don't already explain.

5. Which model should score the trace?

Using the policy model's own log-probabilities is the most natural choice, but it isn't always available, and a small open scorer is much cheaper. How much do boundaries move when the scorer changes? A chunker that works only with the policy's own logits is far less deployable.

6. Agentic traces

Agent transcripts interleave reasoning with tool calls and observations, which are natural boundaries of a different kind. Does a branching signal add anything beyond tool-call structure, and does it find turns within long reasoning segments between calls?

7. Can the signal be gamed?

If a model is ever optimized against a turn-aware monitor, it may learn to smear evidence across boundaries or flatten its own predictive distribution at pivots. Baker et al. already show the broader failure mode: sufficiently strong optimization against a CoT monitor can push the model toward obfuscated reward hacking.2 This deserves a stress test before anyone relies on it.

Limitations

  • Chains of thought aren't guaranteed to be faithful. Chen et al. show that reasoning models often fail to verbalize prompt information that causally changed their answer.10 No text-level segmentation can recover evidence the model never externalized.
  • The boundary score is a heuristic. A broad next-token distribution can mark a real pivot, but it can also reflect an unusual proper noun, formatting choice, or locally ambiguous prose. Predictive uncertainty and semantic importance are not the same quantity.
  • Sentence windows may simply be enough. Their chunk quality is excellent. Adaptive boundaries earn their complexity only if they improve monitoring or localization.
  • The evidence is still diagnostic rather than decisive. The 100-trace audit rates chunk quality, not boundary correctness; the 2,082-trace paired audit measures segmentation statistics, not semantic alignment; and the MMLU analysis is 20 traces from one model.
  • Embedding geometry depends on the representation. Different encoders, context strategies, and projections can distort the path.

Where I land

Chain of thought is an odd interface: part computation, part language, and a complete view of neither. That makes the choice of granularity more consequential than it looks. If the scratchpad unfolds in reasoning moves, it seems worth reading it at the scale of those moves.

To summarize the pieces: sentence windows are a strong baseline. Epistemic words are useful features but poor delimiters. Branching-factor peaks are one cheap candidate for where turns happen. Turn embeddings offer a second, geometric view of how a single trace moves. None of this yet shows that semantic turns make monitors better.

The empirical question is clean. Does a turn-level representation let the same monitor find the important evidence earlier, more precisely, or more robustly than a full rollout or a fixed window? If it does, the chunker has earned a place in the monitoring stack. If it doesn't, the simple baseline wins, and that is worth knowing too.

The implementation is a small Python pipeline: sentence segmentation, prompt-logprob boundary scoring, adaptive peak detection, minimum-length gluing, and optional chunk embeddings. The chunk-quality, paired-segmentation, and MMLU analyses are exploratory; they motivate baselines and hypotheses and are not evidence of improved monitoring. Feedback—especially on the boundary-quality and monitor-ablation experiments—is very welcome.
